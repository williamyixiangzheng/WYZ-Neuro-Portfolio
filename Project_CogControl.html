Hippocampal–cortical maps across modes of adaptive behavioral control

Cornell University · ongoing

Gupta, K., Saito, Y., …, Zheng, Y. W., …, Oliva, A. & Fernandez-Ruiz, A. (2026).

Scientific question

How do animals use memories of past experiences to choose the most beneficial action? This project investigates how spatial memories evolve over time and guide choices during navigation. Rather than treating memory as simply present or absent, we aim to investigate how changes in its strength, content, and accessibility influence behavior.

Abstract

In order to survive in natural environments, animals must exploit learned foraging routes to food sources, while flexibly adapting their strategies upon environmental changes. This adaptive capacity is theorized to depend on cognitive maps: internal representations of spatial, temporal, and abstract relational structures that can be leveraged for guiding behavior. Within the hippocampal–cortical networks, neural representations of these relational variables are often interpreted as the neural substrate for such maps. However, much of the existing literature examines these representations without distinguishing the forms of behavioral control under which such maps are formed and subsequently utilized. Here, we asked how cognitive maps built during habitual navigation in a static environment are modified and repurposed during value-guided patch choice. Rats were trained in a foraging task that dissociated traversal between learned deterministic patches from stay/switch decisions under unpredictable costs into different task phases. We performed large-scale neural ensemble recordings in hippocampal and cortical areas to investigate the underlying neural representations in this task. Behavioral analyses indicated that animals learned stable inter-patch trajectories indicative of procedural behavior and, upon the imposition of variable costs, demonstrated adaptive, value-based decision-making reflecting optimal foraging behavior. Initial neural analyses revealed stability in neural representations across task phases suggesting a shared scaffold of relational structure shared between two modes of behavioral control. Moreover, further analyses demonstrated that such representational stability across task phases cannot be explained by a purely spatial code, but instead is tied to the reward configuration. These findings support a model in which hippocampal–cortical maps are not static allocentric descriptions of space, but behaviorally gated representations that integrate task-relevant variables as animals navigate between different modes of behavioral control.
