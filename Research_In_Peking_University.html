Earlier research at Peking University

Peking University

My first sustained research experience, in molecular and disease neuroscience, in Dr. Chenjian Li's laboratory, which I began while at secondary school in Beijing.

Muscle atrophy and gene-therapy research in Huntington's disease

This project studied gene-therapy approaches in mouse models of Huntington's disease. It introduced me to experimental design, disease models, molecular methods, and animal research, and gave me the surgical and behavioral skills I later carried into systems neuroscience.

My contributions

  • Conducted mouse behavioral assays, including open field, catwalk, grip strength, and rotarod.
  • Performed stereotaxic intracranial AAV injections into striatum and motor cortex.
  • Conducted perfusion, sectioning, and imaging.
  • Used PCR, qPCR, cell culture, SDS-PAGE, plasmid purification, and related molecular methods.
  • Provided experimental support connected to the published study by Dai et al. (2026).

Authorship. I was an experimental contributor to the associated publication and am not an author on it. It is listed on my Publications page under research contributions to other publications, and not among my own publications.

Dai, Y., Abudujielili, Z., Ding, Y., Huang, W., Yin, J., Ou, L., Hu, J., Zheng, S., & Li, C. (2026). Self-inactivating AAV-CRISPR at different ages enables sustained amelioration of Huntington's disease deficits in BAC226Q mice. Science Advances, 12(12), eaea8052. doi:10.1126/sciadv.aea8052

Drosophila O-GlcNAc transferase research

Alongside the mouse work, I studied fly genetics: maintaining stocks, designing mating schemes, screening phenotypes for further crosses and isolating variants, and running island and climbing assays with structured behavioral observation.

Transition to systems and computational neuroscience

This period taught me how experiments are designed and executed, and how much of a result is determined before any analysis begins. It also clarified the questions I wanted to ask. Rather than continuing to study how molecular mechanisms produce disease phenotypes, I became more interested in how populations of neurons represent experience, and I moved into systems and computational neuroscience at Cornell. The experimental training carried over directly: surgery, behavioral work, and histology are the foundation of the electrophysiology projects I work on now.
